    How to use GIKI-Connect

    GIKI-Connect is a course demo about life on a residential campus: small circles by batch or background, and how shared interests and societies can widen your week.

    You fill a short form; the site suggests an interest tribe (one of eight groups learned from the class survey), then shows event ideas and gentle next steps. Treat it like a conversation starter or bulletin board—not a label on your personality and not an official GIK system.

    Use the three tabs like chapters: read here first, try Student second, and open GIKI admin only if you want to practice posting an event for others to see.

    Tap a heading below to expand or collapse that section.

    How to move around the site

    How to use (this tab) — read at your own pace; you can leave and come back.

    Student — scroll from the top through the numbered steps. Move the sliders and tick hobbies so they feel true for you right now, then press Find my interest tribe once. Your result appears further down the same page: which tribe you sit closest to, a map of all tribes (yours is outlined), sample events that fit your interests, and simple ideas for what to try next. The “On this page” row is a shortcut if you want to jump to a section.

    GIKI admin — use this only to play “staff for a minute.” The demo password is already filled in. Write a pretend event (what, when, where), pick hobbies it suits, and optionally pick which tribes should see it near the top of their list. After you publish, go back to Student, run the form again, and check whether your event appears in the suggestions.

    What your tribe result depends on

    When you press Find my interest tribe, the demo compares your answers to eight interest tribes that were worked out once from the course survey. Think of it as “which crowd’s habits are closest to yours this week,” not a grade on your character. These parts of the form matter for that match:

    • The hobbies you tick.
    • How many society hours you say you put in each week (counted as zero if you say you are not in a society).
    • How comfortable you feel meeting students from other provinces.
    • The friendship sliders — together they describe whether your close friends are mostly from the same province or faculty, alongside how many close friends you say you have.

    What does not change the tribe in this demo: faculty, year of study, and the named societies you tick. Those fields are there so your story feels complete on the page and in a report; they are not used to move you into a different tribe unless the project is rebuilt with new rules.

    How this helps you (and what to remember)

    Why it exists. Many students feel their week shrink to the same faces. This demo names that pattern in a friendly way and nudges you toward hobbies, societies, and small plans that are easier to try than a vague “be more social.”

    What you learn from the numbers on Student. The counts at the top remind you that the tribes come from a real class survey (names removed)—so the match is grounded in data, not a random game.

    What you get after you press the button. A tribe label you can compare to the map, a short read on how tight or open your answers look, events that line up with your ticks, and anonymised “people like this cluster” prompts you can treat as icebreakers—not real identities.

    Keep perspective. One run does not define you. Faculty, year, and society names on the form are for your story and the write-up; they do not change the tribe number in this demo. The suggestions are hints; use judgment and follow real campus notices for anything official.

    Interest tribes (for event targeting)

    There are eight tribes, numbered 0 through 7 — same names and headcounts as on the Student tab. Scroll the row of cards to see every tribe before you tick targets below.

    When you publish an event, optional target tribes move it higher on matching students' lists; hobby tags still do the main matching. Cards show a one-line idea for the kind of mixer that fits that tribe.
